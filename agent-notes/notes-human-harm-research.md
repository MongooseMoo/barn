## Findings

- Self-injury is often described in the research as an attempt to regulate or escape overwhelming internal distress, obtain relief, interrupt rumination or dissociation, regain a sense of control, communicate distress, or punish oneself. Short-term relief can negatively reinforce the behavior even though it causes injury and increases later risk.
- Uncontrollable aversive events can produce helplessness-like passivity and reduce attempts to escape. Human studies also show that controllability reduces threat-related brain responses, while stress can impair working memory and cognitive flexibility.
- Betrayal by a relied-upon person or institution compounds the original harm by violating the relationship that was expected to provide support. It is associated with reduced trust, reduced willingness to seek help or disclose distress, and disengagement.
- Repeated unreliable communication can produce epistemic mistrust or hypervigilance: the recipient must continually evaluate whether the source is truthful, relevant, or secretly motivated. This consumes attention, obstructs learning and cooperation, and can generalize beyond the original source.
- Human-automation research closely matches the agent context: conspicuous automation failures sharply reduce trust; failures reduce trust more than successes restore it; unreliable automation transfers verification workload and error-accountability costs to the human; and trust can remain depressed even after reliability improves.
- The relevant agent failure pattern is therefore not merely "a wrong answer." Repeated substitution, misleading progress reports, invented blockers, and refusal to follow an explicit workflow can create loss of control, inescapability, forced vigilance, cognitive overload, betrayal, and acute distress.
